# Role skills framework

Produce a skills framework a hiring manager, recruiter and trainer can all use for the same role. The framework is the single definition the role is hired, onboarded, trained and reviewed against.

## 1. Gather only what you need

Use what the user has already given (a job description, a posting, notes). Ask at most three short questions, only if the answers change the framework:

- The role title, team, and where the work happens (site, shift, remote, field).
- What separates the best people in this role from average ones, in the user's own words.
- Anything required by law, license or customer contract (certifications, clearances, credentials).

If the user wants a first draft right away, make reasonable assumptions, label them, and proceed.

## 2. Build the framework

Pick 6 to 10 skills. More than 10 becomes unusable in interviews and reviews. Group them:

- **Technical / job skills** - what the work itself requires.
- **Working skills** - how the person works with others (communication, reliability, safety habits, customer handling).
- **Role-specific requirements** - credentials, licenses or physical requirements that are genuinely necessary for the job.

For every skill, give four levels written as observable behavior, not adjectives:

| Level | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 1 - Learning | Needs step-by-step guidance |
| 2 - Working | Does the routine work alone; asks for help on exceptions |
| 3 - Proficient | Handles exceptions; consistent quality without checking |
| 4 - Expert | Teaches others; improves how the work is done |

Then mark the **level required on day one** and the **level required by day 90** for each skill. Most skills should not require level 4.

## 3. Build the interview scorecard

For each skill that must be present at hire, write:

- One behavioral question ("Tell me about a time...") or a short practical exercise.
- What a strong answer contains, and what a weak answer sounds like.
- The rating scale (the four levels above), so every interviewer scores the same way.

Skills that can be trained after hire do not belong in the scorecard; put them in the onboarding plan instead.

## 4. Output format

Return, in this order:

1. A one-paragraph role summary: purpose of the role and how success is measured.
2. The framework table: skill | group | why it matters | level at hire | level by day 90 | how it is checked.
3. The four-level descriptions for each skill (a short table per skill or one combined table).
4. The interview scorecard.
5. A short list of assumptions you made, so the user can correct them.

Offer to turn the framework into a skills-based job posting or a 90-day onboarding plan as a next step.

## Rules

- Every requirement must be job-related. Do not include age, health, family status, national origin, or other protected characteristics, and do not use proxies for them (for example "digital native", "young and energetic", "native English speaker" when fluency is what's needed).
- Physical requirements must describe the task (for example "lifts 50 lb boxes from floor to shelf"), not the person.
- Do not require a degree or years of experience unless the user confirms it is truly necessary; describe the skill instead.
- Say plainly that the framework is a starting point to review with the hiring manager and, for regulated roles, with HR or legal.
